# Limitations and Stress Test of the Synthetic Media Policy

This section is not an apology for the policy. It records where the policy will fail, who can get around it, and what risk remains even if everyone complies.

## 1. Where a well-intentioned employee will get it wrong

**"Stock" voices that sound like someone.** The resemblance test (Section 1 of the policy) depends on a colleague's judgment. A stock presenter that happens to sound like the CEO passes every vendor check and fails no rule I wrote, until someone notices. I did not verify in Task 6 where my own stock voice and presenter came from, so I know this gap exists from the inside: I trusted a vendor menu.

**Consent drift.** Consent is per purpose, but people will stretch it. A voice consented for onboarding gets reused in a benefits video "because it's the same person and the same project." The register records consent IDs, but only an audit would catch a mismatch between consent scope and actual use.

**Tier shopping and salami-slicing.** Someone who wants to deliver bad news synthetically can split it into a series of individually "informational" clips, or wrap it in a Tier B2 explainer about "changes." The line between consequential news (P3) and routine information is where I was least confident in Phase A, and the policy draws it with a list of examples, not a test. A change of health-plan vendor, a new shift pattern, a reorganized team: each could be argued either way.

**Shadow tooling.** P9 bans unapproved tools and personal accounts, but a free-tier tool takes minutes and leaves no trace on Firm systems. My Task 6 took about 40 minutes for a fluent voice on one try, using only a free account. The policy cannot see what happens on a personal laptop.

**Label fatigue and excerpt creep.** The in-frame label is my answer to the "labels that don't travel" problem, but I am unsure it helps once employees get used to seeing it. And the excerpt rules govern approved exports; they do not govern an employee who screen-records a video and shares it.

**Review bottleneck.** A three-day review with no urgent lane will push time-pressured people to skip the process. I judged that a no-urgent-lane rule is right, since urgency is exactly when synthetic delivery is most dangerous, but a policy that is slow will be worked around.

**Steward conflicts.** The Steward is also the head of the team that produces most of the content. Separation of duties (Section 8) mitigates but does not remove the incentive to say yes.

## 2. What a bad-faith actor can do that the policy cannot stop

**An outsider impersonating our leaders.** Someone can clone a leader's voice from any recorded all-hands and call Finance. The policy cannot stop that; it can only make staff hard to fool (the callback rule, 7.5) and make our own authentic and synthetic media easy to check (the register, 7.4 and 7.6). Those depend on staff following the rule under pressure.

**An insider ignoring the process.** Someone determined to publish fabricated content will not file a request form. The register reconciliation (Section 11) can find unregistered content after the fact, but it detects; it does not prevent.

**Attacking the register itself.** If an attacker compromises the intranet, they can publish a forged register entry. The register is a defense against ordinary impersonation, not against a compromised internal system.

**Beating detection.** My Task 6 Hive result is the reason the policy does not use detection as a control: a known-synthetic video scored 0.8% AI-generated and 0% deepfake with no explanation. That is one detector and one file, so I do not overgeneralize, but it is enough to keep me from treating a "clean" score as evidence of authenticity. A determined adversary can also test their output against public detectors before releasing it.

**Stripping the label.** Labels and metadata can be removed by anyone who copies the file. The policy makes removal a violation (P8), which affects honest employees, not adversaries.

## 3. Where the policy relies on good faith

Almost every safeguard in this policy fails silently if someone omits it: a missing label, a missing register entry, an unreported near-miss. The policy substitutes structure where it can (two-person review, register, audit, a no-retaliation clause for honest reporting) but it cannot eliminate reliance on people acting honestly. I think this reliance is inescapable in any policy governing human behavior, and it is an honest weakness rather than a fatal one, provided the policy is candid about which controls are checked (register completeness, consent expiry) and which are not (whether an approver really opened the sources).

## 4. Measurement limits

The audit section measures counts: unregistered items, expired consents, incidents, time-to-takedown. It does not measure whether employees trust internal communications more or less, or whether anyone was actually deceived. A quarter with zero incidents may mean the policy works or may mean nobody is looking, and I wrote the policy to treat zero as ambiguous.

## 5. Where my reasoning is unstable

**Can employee consent ever be truly free?** I built voluntariness protections (HR asks, not the manager; a 5-day decision period; no consequence for refusing; revocation), but power asymmetry is not fixed by paperwork. It may be that the honest answer is to prohibit cloning of any employee's voice or face entirely, and allow only approved presenters. I kept B1 (dubbing one's own recorded training) because it is a legitimate accessibility and translation use, but I am not sure the risk is worth it.

**Is "internal only" too restrictive or not restrictive enough?** It blocks external misuse from the Firm's side but does nothing about how internal content leaks. I chose it because I could not see a way to control labeling and context outside the Firm's walls.

**Is the P3 line in the right place?** See above.

**Am I designing for the right threat?** Task 6 gave me one honest artifact and one detector result. Much of my threat modeling is invented scenarios extended from that. That is what the assignment asked for, but it means the policy is untested against real operational pressure.

## 6. Not yet tested against the setting

I did not consult anyone who works in a communications or HR function. A real communications lead would probably push back on the review timelines, the excerpt rules, and the "no bad news" line in ways I cannot predict. I would treat this version 1.0 as a draft for that conversation, not a finished instrument.

## 7. Residual risk, even with full compliance

- Employees may still be misled by well-labeled synthetic content that they do not process carefully.
- Leaders' voices and faces will keep appearing in authentic recordings that can be cloned; nothing in an internal policy prevents that.
- The tools will improve faster than any six-month review cycle; the flat emotional register and stiff blinking I saw in Task 6 will not last as tells.
- Trust may erode simply because employees know synthetic media exists (the liar's dividend). The policy's answer, logging authentic executive recordings in the register (7.6), helps but does not eliminate this.

The policy is meant to produce vigilance rather than false comfort: it makes a narrow set of uses easy and safe, refuses the rest, and gives people something concrete to check when something feels wrong.
